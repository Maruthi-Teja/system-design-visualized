Design a Notification System

One API that any service can call to reach a user by push, SMS, email or in-app — respecting their preferences, never sending the same OTP twice, and never letting a 20-million-user marketing blast delay a password reset.

  • 1B notifications / day
  • priority isolation
  • idempotency keys
  • backoff + jitter
  • at-least-once
01

Requirements

Two very different workloads share this system: small, urgent transactional messages and huge, patient bulk campaigns. Say so up front — most of the design is about keeping them out of each other’s way.

Functional

  • Multi-channel — push (iOS / Android), SMS, email, in-app inbox.
  • Event-driven API — callers send a type + data; we render the text.
  • Preferences — opt-outs per category and channel, quiet hours, language.
  • Bulk campaigns — one request to millions of users, scheduled by time zone.
  • Frequency caps — e.g. at most 3 promotional pushes a day.
  • Status tracking — sent, delivered, opened, bounced.

Non-functional

  • Fast for critical — OTPs and security alerts out in a few seconds, even during a campaign.
  • Throughput — ~1B / day, bursts of 100K+/s.
  • Reliable — at-least-once; never silently drop a transactional message.
  • No duplicates — caller retries must not send twice.
  • Respect provider limits — and survive provider outages.
  • Status can lag — eventual consistency is fine for tracking.

Out of scope

  • Building a push or SMS network — we use providers
  • Campaign authoring UI and audience building
  • Rich email editor and A/B test analysis
  • Real-time chat (that’s a different system)
  • Billing for SMS costs

Scaling

Stateless Every stage scales alone

API, dispatcher and channel workers hold no state of their own; queues sit between each stage. Scale each on its own queue lag: a campaign scales dispatchers, an email backlog scales email workers only.

Providers The real bottleneck

Your fleet can always grow; provider quotas can’t. Rate-limit per provider and per sender account with token buckets, reserve a share for critical traffic, and keep a second SMS and email provider for failover.

Order Per user, not global

Partitioning by user_id keeps one user’s notifications in order (“shipped” before “delivered”) while spreading load. Global ordering is neither possible nor needed. Collapse keys handle updates that overtake each other.

Interviewer asks…

Likely follow-ups. Try answering out loud before you expand each one.

Q1How do you make sure a user never gets the same OTP twice?

DUPLICATES

Duplicates come from two places, and each needs its own fix:

  • Caller retries (the API call timed out but succeeded): an Idempotency-Key stored with SET NX. A repeat returns the original notification_id and sends nothing.
  • Internal retries (a worker crashed after the provider accepted): check the delivery log for sent before calling the provider, and pass message_id to the provider where it supports deduplication or collapse keys.

Be honest about the limit: with an external provider in the loop, the guarantee is at-least-once with a very small duplicate window, not exactly-once. A good OTP design tolerates that — the second copy carries the same code.

Q2Marketing just sent to 20M users. How do you stop that from delaying a password reset?

PRIORITY ISOLATION

Isolation at every shared resource, not just a priority field:

  • Separate topics per priority with separate consumer pools and reserved capacity for critical.
  • Separate channel queues per priority, so bulk email can’t back up the critical email queue.
  • Provider quota split: reserve, say, 30% of each provider’s rate limit for critical.
  • Throttled fan-out: the campaign is expanded at a fixed rate instead of all at once.
  • Back-pressure: if critical consumer lag rises, pause bulk consumers automatically.
Q3The SMS provider is down for 10 minutes. Walk me through what happens.

PROVIDER OUTAGE

Workers see timeouts and 5xx errors. Messages go to the retry queue with exponential backoff and jitter. After a handful of failures in a short window the circuit breaker opens: workers stop calling that provider at all and fail fast.

  • Critical SMS fails over to the secondary provider straight away.
  • Normal traffic waits and retries; messages past their expires_at (an OTP after 5 minutes) are dropped to the DLQ rather than delivered uselessly late.
  • The breaker goes half-open periodically, sending a trickle; when those succeed, traffic ramps back gradually — with jitter, so the backlog doesn’t knock the provider over again.
Q4Why exponential backoff with jitter? Isn’t backoff enough?

RETRIES

Backoff reduces how often each message retries. It does nothing about when: messages that failed together retry together, because they all compute the same delays. When the provider recovers it’s hit by one synchronised wave, overloads, and the cycle repeats.

Jitter randomises each delay (full jitter: a random time between 0 and the backoff), turning the wave into a steady stream. The retry lab above shows it: 1,000 retries in one second without jitter, under a hundred per second with it.

Q5How do quiet hours and “send at 9 AM local time” work across time zones?

SCHEDULING

Store each user’s IANA time zone in preferences. For a scheduled campaign, the fan-out service groups the audience by time zone and enqueues each group when its local 9 AM arrives — the load spreads itself across 24 hours.

For quiet hours, the dispatcher checks the user’s local time; non-critical messages are put on a scheduled queue (a time-bucketed store or delayed topic) with send_at = end of quiet hours. Critical messages ignore quiet hours. Watch for daylight-saving transitions: compute with a time-zone library, never with fixed UTC offsets.

Q6How do you keep users from being spammed by many different teams?

FREQUENCY CAPS

Because every team goes through one system, it can enforce global per-user caps by category: e.g. at most 3 promotional pushes per day, at most 1 per hour. The dispatcher increments a counter per (user, category, window) in Redis and drops or digests anything over the cap.

Transactional and critical categories are exempt — you never cap a password reset. Collapse similar notifications (“5 people liked your post”) instead of sending five.

Q7How do you know whether a notification was actually delivered or read?

TRACKING

  • Sent: the provider accepted it — recorded by our worker.
  • Delivered: provider receipts / webhooks for SMS and email; push providers give limited delivery data, so the app itself can report receipt.
  • Opened / clicked: tracking pixels and redirect links in email; the app reports opens for push and in-app.

All of these arrive late, duplicated and out of order. Store status as a forward-only state machine, deduplicate by (message_id, event), and aggregate in an analytics store for campaign reports.

Q8Does order matter? What if “delivered” arrives before “shipped”?

ORDERING

Per-user order matters; global order doesn’t. Partition every queue by user_id so one user’s notifications are processed in sequence by one consumer at a time.

Retries can still reorder things (“shipped” fails and retries after “delivered” succeeds). Two cheap defences: give each notification an expires_at or a sequence number per entity and drop stale ones, and use the same collapse key (e.g. order-981) so a newer status replaces the older one on the device.

Q9Where does the in-app inbox fit, if you already have push?

IN-APP

Push is unreliable by design — phones are offline, users disable it, the OS drops it. The inbox is the durable record: written by the dispatcher like any other channel, stored per user, and synced by the app on open (paginated by created_at, with unread counts).

Push then becomes a doorbell — “you have something new” — and the app fetches the content. That also keeps sensitive details off the lock screen.

Q10Where are the bottlenecks, and what would you monitor?

OPERATIONS

The internal stages scale horizontally; the constraints are provider quotas, preference lookups during big campaigns, and hot users (a celebrity account generating notifications for millions of followers — that is a fan-out problem, handled like a campaign).

Monitor:

  • Consumer lag per priority topic — the earliest warning that critical is slowing down.
  • End-to-end latency for critical (request accepted → provider accepted), p50 / p99.
  • Provider error and throttle rates, circuit-breaker state, DLQ growth.
  • Opt-out and spam-complaint rates per campaign — the business signal that you’re over-sending.
